The trays were ordered two months ago. The cheongsam is tailored, the restaurant is paid for, and Mum has checked the gift list three times. Then, the night before the sangjit (the Chinese Indonesian betrothal, when the groom's family brings trays of gifts to the bride's), someone asks in the family group chat: "What are we doing for music tomorrow?" What happens next is nearly always the same. A cousin opens YouTube, types "Mandarin songs", and the next afternoon *Gong Xi Gong Xi* is playing as the groom's family walks in. It's cheerful, everybody knows it, and it's a Lunar New Year song.

Nobody will be upset. But a sangjit is the day two extended families sit in one room together, formally, for the first time, and the right music makes that room feel warmer without anyone noticing why. This article lines the songs up against the order of the ceremony, so you only need to build one playlist and hand it to one person.

## A sangjit happens at midday, and the audience is the parents

Those two facts decide almost every song choice. A sangjit is usually held late in the morning, at home or in a restaurant's private room, and most of the guests are immediate family and senior relatives. There's no dance floor. People come to shake hands, look at the trays, eat, and talk for a long time.

So the music here is background. Keep the volume at a level where people at the same table can talk without leaning in. Pick songs the parents' and grandparents' generation will recognise, because they're the ones who sit the longest and notice the most. Whatever is trending on social media can go in, as long as it's a small share.

## Act one: waiting for the groom's family

The bride's family is usually ready half an hour to an hour before the groom's party arrives. It's a busy stretch: someone is straightening the table, someone is on the phone asking where the cars are, an aunt has just arrived and needs greeting. At this point, songs with lyrics only compete with the conversation.

Go instrumental. Guzheng and erhu set a Chinese mood without a single word, and piano versions of Teresa Teng's songs are easy to find on any streaming service. Search "guzheng wedding instrumental" or "Teresa Teng piano" and you'll have more than enough for an hour. Listen to a few minutes before you add anything, because some guzheng recordings are extremely slow and belong in a spa, not at a family celebration.

## Act two: the trays come in

This is the moment everyone photographs. The groom's party files in carrying red trays while the bride's family greets them at the door. The music should lift a little here: bright, mid-tempo, and with lyrics that are actually about getting married.

The safest choice is **Jin Tian Ni Yao Jia Gei Wo** (今天你要嫁给我) by David Tao and Jolin Tsai. The title means "today you're marrying me", and almost anyone who has been to a Chinese wedding in the last fifteen years has heard it. A good second song is **Zan Men Jie Hun Ba** (咱们结婚吧) by Qi Chen, which works a snatch of the Wedding March into its melody.

Two songs are enough for this act. The trays rarely take more than ten minutes to come in, and after that people need to hear the host.

## Act three: the handover and the jewellery

Once the trays are set down and the bride-to-be comes out, the room goes quieter. There are short words from both families, and then the jewellery is put on her. Turn the volume down and choose gentle love songs.

**Yue Liang Dai Biao Wo De Xin** (月亮代表我的心) by Teresa Teng is almost never out of place. The lyric is simple: you ask how deeply I love you; look at the moon, it speaks for my heart. Also from Teresa Teng, **Tian Mi Mi** (甜蜜蜜) comes with a story Indonesian families enjoy. Its melody was borrowed from one of our own folk songs, *Dayung Sampan*. If an uncle starts humming along with Indonesian words, he hasn't got the wrong song.

For couples who want something from their own generation, **Wo Yuan Yi** (我愿意) by Faye Wong means "I'm willing", and **Zui Lang Man De Shi** (最浪漫的事) by Cyndi Chao is about the most romantic thing imaginable, which turns out to be growing old slowly with the same person.

## Act four: the trays go back, then everyone eats

After the bride's family returns part of what was in the trays, the formal part is over and everyone moves to the table. This is the longest stretch, often more than an hour, and it's where songs for the parents' generation belong.

Many Chinese Indonesian families don't speak Mandarin at home. They speak Hokkien, Hakka or Teochew, and the songs that make the grandfathers and grandmothers smile are very often Hokkien ones. **Ai Pia Cia E Ia** (爱拼才会赢) isn't really a love song. It's about never giving up, but nearly every parent knows the chorus and will sing along. **Ke Au** (家后) by Jody Chiang goes deeper: a wife speaks to her husband about staying beside him into old age. Play it once the mood is relaxed, and don't be surprised if a few eyes go glassy.

For families with Cantonese roots, **Shang Hai Tan** (上海滩) by Frances Yip is instant nostalgia. As guests start to leave, **Peng You** (朋友) by Wakin Chau closes things nicely. It's a song about friendship, and at plenty of Chinese family banquets the whole room sings it together.

## The running order at a glance

| Act | Mood | Songs |
|---|---|---|
| Waiting for the groom's family | Busy, lots of chatter | Guzheng, erhu or piano instrumentals |
| The trays come in | Bright, everyone taking photos | Jin Tian Ni Yao Jia Gei Wo, Zan Men Jie Hun Ba |
| Handover and jewellery | Calm, a little solemn | Yue Liang Dai Biao Wo De Xin, Tian Mi Mi, Wo Yuan Yi, Zui Lang Man De Shi |
| Lunch together | Relaxed, long | Ai Pia Cia E Ia, Ke Au, Shang Hai Tan, more Teresa Teng |
| Guests leaving | Warm | Peng You |

## What to leave off the playlist

Lunar New Year songs like *Gong Xi Gong Xi* sound festive, but to the older generation they belong to one particular season. It's a bit like playing Christmas carols at an engagement party.

What slips through more often is the heartbreak song with a sweet melody. **Xiao Xing Yun** (小幸运) and **Hou Lai** (后来) are hugely popular and lovely to listen to, yet both are about a love that has already gone. Relatives who understand Mandarin will catch the words. We cover songs like these at more length in our article on Mandarin songs for weddings.

## The practical bits people forget

Put one person in charge of the music, ideally a cousin or friend who has no other job that day. The couple won't have a hand free for a phone.

Download the playlist so it plays offline. Reception in a restaurant's private room is often poor, and a free streaming account will drop an advert right in the middle of the jewellery. If you're in a restaurant, ask when you book whether the room has its own sound system and which cable it needs. At home, one portable speaker placed well away from the main table is plenty.

Arrange the songs in the order of the acts, then tell your music person when to move on. The cues are easy: the groom's family gets out of the cars, the bride-to-be comes out, and the food starts arriving.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a sangjit have to use Mandarin songs?

There's no customary rule about music at all. Sangjit in earlier generations went ahead with none. Mandarin and Hokkien songs are used because they set the mood and please the older relatives. If your family is more at home with Indonesian songs or hymns, that's a perfectly valid choice.

### Nobody in our family speaks Mandarin. Is it still worth it?

Songs like *Yue Liang Dai Biao Wo De Xin* are recognised by their melody. You can go fully instrumental and the feeling will still be there.

### Do we need live music?

For a sangjit at home or in a private room, rarely. The room is small and the ceremony itself is short. If you'd still like some, a single guzheng player or violinist fits the scale far better than a full band.

### How many songs should we prepare?

Work it out from the running time. A two-hour event needs roughly thirty songs. Prepare extra, because the groom's party may run late and a big family lunch almost always goes on longer than planned.

## Next steps

Ask both sets of parents which one song they'd like to hear that day. The answers are often a surprise, and putting that song on the playlist is the easiest way to make them feel included. After that, sort your songs into the five acts above, download everything, and hand it to your music person at least three days ahead so they can try it on the speaker you'll actually use.
