You've read three articles about what goes on the trays at a sangjit, the Chinese Indonesian betrothal when the groom's family brings gifts to the bride's, and you feel you've got it. Then your future mother-in-law asks, "Where's the sugarcane?" Not one of those articles mentioned sugarcane. In your own family, your sister's sangjit had none, nobody felt anything was missing, and your mum is as puzzled as you are.

Nobody here is wrong. Your family and your partner's most likely come from different groups. Chinese Indonesians descend from several regions of southern China, each with its own language and habits: Hokkien, Teochew (Tiociu), Hakka (Khek), Cantonese (Kanton or Konghu), and Hainanese. The outline of the sangjit is the same. The details aren't.

## Where these notes come from

In Indonesia, the differences between the groups rarely get written down. The knowledge lives in the heads of aunts and grandfathers, and many families have intermarried for three or four generations, so the traditions have blended too.

Singapore is another story. The same ceremony is called **Guo Da Li** (过大礼) there, and wedding supply shops sell it as separate packages for each dialect group, complete with item lists. Since the ancestors of Chinese Singaporeans and Chinese Indonesians came from the same regions, those lists make a useful reference. Treat this article as a general map. What applies in your family is still decided by its elders.

## What every group has in common

Before the differences, here's the framework nearly every family uses:

- Red envelopes from the groom's side, known here as uang susu (milk money) and uang pesta (banquet money)
- A pair of red candles with a dragon and a phoenix
- Oranges and apples in even numbers
- Wedding cakes
- Rice wine or wine
- Jewellery for the bride-to-be
- Dried fruits and seeds, each carrying a wish: dried longan, red dates, lotus seeds, walnuts and lily bulbs

If your list covers all of this, you're safe in any family. The differences between groups are additions on top of that framework.

## Hokkien and Teochew: similar, with a few markers

These two groups were neighbours in their homeland and their lists are almost the same. Both commonly bring pig trotters, these days often canned, or roast pork.

The marker of a **Hokkien** family is **sugarcane**. The story goes back a long way, to a time when the ancestors of the Hokkien survived a massacre by hiding in sugarcane fields. Ever since, sugarcane has stood for protection. This is the sugarcane your future mother-in-law was looking for. In many families a pair of sugarcane stalks also turns up again among the return gifts. Besides sugarcane, Hokkien families usually add rice candy as a wish for prosperity.

A **Teochew** family has two markers. The first is sweets: peanut candy and sesame candy, plus bananas as a wish for children soon. The second, and the better known, is **Si Dian Jin** (四点金), the four pieces of gold. The set is a necklace, a bangle, a pair of earrings and a ring. By tradition it's given by the groom's mother, as a promise that her daughter-in-law is welcome in that home and will always be looked after. Some people read the four as the four needs of life: clothing, food, shelter and transport.

Si Dian Jin is interesting because it's the one place where the number four is worn with pride, in a tradition that otherwise avoids it.

## Cantonese and Hakka: dried seafood and roast pig

The lists for **Cantonese** and **Hakka** families also resemble each other, and their hallmark is **dried seafood**. Sea cucumber, abalone, dried scallops, dried cuttlefish, dried prawns, dried oysters, mushrooms and fish maw all stand for a long and happy marriage. This is usually the most expensive tray, and Cantonese families tend to care that it's complete.

The other Cantonese markers are **a pair of dragon and phoenix bangles** for the bride, and **a whole roast pig**. In the old tradition a whole roast pig carried a meaning of its own, to do with the bride's honour. These days most families order it as part of the package without attaching that meaning to it.

A **Hakka** family sometimes adds a dish of its own. In Singapore the one most often mentioned is suan pan zi, a yam snack shaped like abacus beads, standing for a head for figures and ever-growing fortune. Some Hakka families in Indonesia also have a custom of swapping trays: a tray of the bride's everyday things is exchanged for a tray of the groom's.

## Hainanese: shorter

**Hainanese** families are known for liking a simple ceremony. The list is shorter, and some families leave out Si Dian Jin altogether. If your partner's family is Hainanese and the list from their side looks brief, that's simply their style.

## The differences at a glance

| Group | Distinctive additions | Jewellery |
|---|---|---|
| Hokkien | Sugarcane, rice candy, pig trotters | A pair of bangles, any design |
| Teochew | Peanut and sesame candy, bananas, pig trotters | Si Dian Jin, four pieces of gold from the mother-in-law |
| Cantonese | Dried seafood, whole roast pig | Dragon and phoenix bangles |
| Hakka | Dried seafood, a Hakka speciality dish | Similar to Cantonese |
| Hainanese | A shorter list | Often no Si Dian Jin |

## When the two families come from different groups

The traditional rule is simple: **follow the groom's family's custom**. A sangjit is a gift from the groom's side, so they decide what goes on the trays.

In practice the rule is rarely applied that strictly. The bride's family has hopes too. A Teochew mother who once received Si Dian Jin can reasonably hope her daughter will receive the same. Some routes couples commonly take:

1. **Use the groom's side's list as the base, then add one or two markers from the bride's side.** Adding sugarcane or sesame candy costs little, and it means a great deal to parents who see their own tradition present.
2. **Ask first, don't guess.** Have both sets of parents write down what they think must be there. The list is usually shorter than you fear.
3. **Discuss the jewellery separately.** Si Dian Jin and dragon-phoenix bangles are the biggest line items, and they've now been widely adopted across groups. Decide together whether to have them, and who buys.

## What if the family doesn't know its group

This happens more than people assume. After several generations in Indonesia, many families only know that they're Chinese. There are a few clues. The language the grandparents speak at home is the clearest. The family's home city helps as well: Medan and most of Java are largely home to Hokkien descendants, Pontianak is known for Teochew, and Singkawang, Bangka and Belitung are known for Hakka. The kinship terms used at home also differ between groups.

If you still can't find out, that's fine. Use the general framework above. No aunt is going to object to trays that are complete and neatly arranged.

## What the bride's family gives back

In every group, the bride's family doesn't simply accept. Part of what's on the trays is returned, roughly half, as a sign that the proposal is accepted and the good fortune is shared. A pair of candles goes back as well. Along with that, the bride's family gives gifts to the groom-to-be. The usual ones are a watch, a wallet or a belt, with a red envelope tucked inside. Hokkien families often add steamed huat kueh cakes and sugarcane.

As for the money, practice varies from one family to the next. Some keep all of the banquet money, and some return most of it. Talk about it before the day so nobody is caught off guard in front of guests.

## So where does tingjing fit in

Some families know one more stage before the sangjit, the tingjing, a simpler engagement to seal the promise. Many families now combine it with the sangjit or skip it. If your parents use the word, ask what they have in mind, because what it involves differs from one family to another.

## Frequently asked questions

### My partner isn't Chinese. Whose list do we use?

If it's the groom who isn't Chinese, the bride's family usually helps put the list together, often in a simplified version. If it's the bride who isn't Chinese, the sangjit can still go ahead as an introduction to the tradition, with a short explanation for her family beforehand.

### Our family is Muslim or doesn't eat pork. What about the pig trotters and the roast pig?

Just swap them. Many families use chicken, or add more fruit and cakes. The meaning lies in abundance and goodwill. The kind of meat can be adapted.

### Do we have to buy every item ourselves?

No. In the big cities there are shops and gift-tray services that offer sangjit packages. Tell them your family's group when you order, and ask for the contents in writing so your parents can check them.

### When should the sangjit be held?

Practice varies, from two weeks to a few months before the wedding, on a date calculated to be auspicious.

## Next steps

Ask each set of parents two things: which group is our family from, and which items do they feel simply can't be missing. Merge the two answers into one list, show it to both families, and get their agreement before buying anything.
