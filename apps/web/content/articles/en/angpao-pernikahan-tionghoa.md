The night before a sangjit, the Chinese Indonesian betrothal when the groom's family brings gift trays to the bride's home, the dining table at the groom's house is usually covered in red envelopes. Mum is writing names on each one, Dad is counting crisp notes from the bank, and someone asks in the family group chat, "Do we prepare the ones for the tray bearers, or does their side?" Nobody's sure. Everyone remembers seeing them, but nobody remembers who brought them.

Angpao, the Indonesian spelling of what Singaporeans call ang bao, come in more than one kind at a Chinese wedding. Some go from the groom's family to the bride's, some travel the other way, some go from parents to the couple, and some come from guests. This article maps who gives to whom, what written sources say about the amounts, and where your own family's habits have the final say.

## The basic rule: even, and avoid four

Nearly every source agrees on one thing. For happy occasions, the amount is even. IDX Channel, in a 2024 explainer, wrote that odd numbers are reserved for deaths, and that four is taboo even though it's even, because it sounds close to the word for "death". Gramedia Literasi adds that the banquet money should contain the digits 8 and 9, read as luck and permanence.

So if someone asks whether a wedding angpao should be odd or even, the answer is even. We cover the sound logic behind each number in our article on [lucky numbers and taboos in Chinese Indonesian weddings](https://7magicwedding.com/artikel/tradisi-wedding/angka-baik-dan-pantangan-pernikahan-tionghoa), including why amounts ending in 88 or 99 are so popular.

One more custom, noted by Rinto Jiang at Budaya Tionghoa: the people who give angpao are generally those who are already married. That's why, at a wedding, angpao mostly flow from parents and older relatives to the couple, and from the newly married couple to younger, unmarried siblings.

## Angpao at the sangjit: who gives to whom

The sangjit is when the groom's family arrives at the bride's family home with trays of betrothal gifts. This is where the most angpao change hands, and where the sources disagree most often.

**Milk money (uang susu).** Given by the groom's family to the bride's parents as thanks for raising their daughter. Gramedia Literasi and Popbela (April 2025) both describe it this way. Where they differ is how much is kept. Tionghoa.info (Herman Tan, updated 2021) says the bride's side usually keeps the milk money in full. Popbela says the bride's parents keep half or all of it. Indonesian Wikipedia says only a small part is kept. All three turn up in real life, and which version applies is decided by the bride's parents.

**Banquet money (uang pesta).** Gramedia Literasi also calls it uang mahar, a dowry. It's meant as the groom's family's contribution to the cost of the celebration. Here the sources are more consistent: the bride's side keeps only the "tail" and returns the rest. Tionghoa.info gives the example of Rp13,000,000 in banquet money of which only Rp3,000,000 is kept. Popbela gives Rp3,030,000 of which only Rp30,000 is kept, and adds that some families match the figure to the bride's age. If the bride's family keeps all of the banquet money, it means they're paying for the celebration. These examples show how the counting works, not how much to give.

**Envelopes for the tray bearers.** This is the answer to the group-chat question. According to Tionghoa.info and Gramedia Literasi, these are prepared by the bride's family and handed to the groom's tray bearers, usually by the bride's mother. The wish is that the single ones among them will soon follow.

**Other envelopes, by family custom.** Popbela mentions "nappy money" (uang popok) and "sibling money" (uang saudara), which only some families observe.

## So what are the "four sangjit angpao"?

Lots of people search for a list of four sangjit angpao, and we understand why: a checklist feels reassuring. The trouble is that we found no written source that fixes the number at exactly four. Pulling together the sources above, the four most often mentioned are the milk money, the banquet money, the envelopes for the tray bearers, and one extra envelope whose form depends on the family, such as nappy money or sibling money.

Tionghoa.info itself stresses that sangjit contents vary from family to family and follow regional custom. Ancestry matters too. Hokkien, Teochew, Hakka and Cantonese families have different gift customs, as we set out in our article on [sangjit by dialect group](https://7magicwedding.com/artikel/tradisi-wedding/sangjit-hokkien-khek-tiociu-kanton-beda-aturan). If your future in-laws mention "four angpao", simply ask what they mean by it. That question is far safer than guessing.

## How much goes in the milk money?

There's no fixed figure. Gramedia Literasi says the amount is open and depends on what the groom's side can afford, and Popbela also describes the milk money as an open amount. What almost always matters is the shape of the number: even, no four, and often with an 8 or a 9.

Because the size is left to agreement, the approach that most often keeps the peace is to discuss it beforehand. Usually that happens through the parents, or through one relative both sides trust, a few weeks before the sangjit. At the same time, agree how much of the banquet money will be returned, so nobody feels awkward when the envelopes are opened.

## Tea ceremony: from elders to the couple

At the tea ceremony, or tea pai, the couple serve tea to their parents and older relatives as a mark of respect. Once the tea is accepted, the elders give an angpao or jewellery as a blessing, as we explain in our article on [the tea pai tradition](https://7magicwedding.com/artikel/tradisi-wedding/tea-pai-tradisi-teh-pernikahan-tionghoa-yang-penuh-makna-filosofi). SingaporeBrides' tea ceremony guide (2020) describes the same pattern, plus one in the other direction: younger siblings and nieces and nephews who pay their respects receive a red packet from the couple.

So if you're the couple, prepare for two sets of envelopes: the ones you receive from your elders, and the ones you give to the younger ones. The second set is the one most often forgotten. If you need music for this part of the day, our list is in the article on [music for sungkeman and tea pai](https://7magicwedding.com/artikel/tradisi-wedding/sungkeman-dan-tea-pai-lagu-pengiring).

## Angpao from reception guests

For guests, the principle is the same: even, and avoid four. In Indonesia, we found no dated source that publishes a reference amount for guests at a Chinese wedding, so we won't give one. The usual practice is to match the amount to how close you are to the couple and what you can afford.

## Angpao in Jakarta, ang bao in Singapore

In Singapore, the word is ang bao, and there's one big difference: the reference amounts are published openly. SingaporeBrides publishes a list of wedding ang bao rates by venue, last updated on 12 October 2026. The rule of thumb is that guests who aren't especially close to the couple should at least cover the cost of one seat at a table of ten. Close family or old friends, according to the same guide, may add 20 percent or more if they want to help with the couple's costs.

A few examples from that list, for a Saturday dinner:

| Singapore venue | Ang bao per guest, Saturday dinner |
|---|---|
| 1-Alfaro | S$210 |
| 1-Altitude Coast | S$260 |
| Flower Field Hall, Gardens by the Bay | S$280 to S$330 |
| Raffles Singapore | S$310 to S$340 |

These figures are SingaporeBrides', not a 7Magic guideline, and the source says they may change without notice. The same guide also says four is best avoided and eight is considered lucky, just as in Indonesia.

If you're a Jakarta guest at a Singapore wedding, or the other way round, the safest approach is to follow the custom of the place where the celebration is held. If your two families come from two countries and you're planning a sangjit, differences like this usually show up elsewhere too, not only in the envelopes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should a wedding angpao be an odd or even amount?

Even. Odd amounts are associated with funerals, and four is avoided even though it's even.

### Who prepares the envelopes for the tray bearers?

According to Tionghoa.info and Gramedia Literasi, the bride's family. The envelopes go to the groom's tray bearers at the end of the visit.

### Is the milk money returned?

It depends on the family. The sources we read differ: some say it's kept in full, some say half or all, some say only a small part. Ask the bride's parents before the sangjit day.

### Who handles the envelopes if we use a sangjit vendor?

The angpao contents, the milk money and the jewellery are still prepared by the family.
