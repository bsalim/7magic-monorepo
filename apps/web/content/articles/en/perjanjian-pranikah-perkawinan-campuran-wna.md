In 2012, Ike Farida, a Jakarta lawyer, paid in full for an apartment in South Jakarta. The developer then cancelled the sale. Her husband is Japanese, they had no marriage agreement, and under Indonesian law that meant part of anything she bought also belonged to him. She took the case to the Constitutional Court, and part of the law changed because of it.

If you are a foreigner about to marry an Indonesian, or an Indonesian about to marry a foreigner, this is the part of wedding planning nobody at the venue will bring up. In most countries a prenup is about what happens if you divorce. In Indonesia, for a mixed couple, it mostly decides something much closer to home: whether the Indonesian partner can keep owning land, a house or an apartment in their own name.

## What happens to property without a prenup

Article 35 of Indonesia's Marriage Law makes everything acquired during the marriage joint property, unless the couple agrees otherwise. Between two Indonesians that is unremarkable. For a mixed couple it collides with the Basic Agrarian Law (UUPA), which reserves freehold (Hak Milik) and right-to-build (Hak Guna Bangunan) titles for Indonesian citizens.

Article 21(3) of the UUPA spells out the consequence. A foreigner who acquires freehold through the mixing of marital property must give it up within one year. If they don't, the right is extinguished by law and the land falls to the state. Because a half share of joint property already counts, the safe position for banks, developers and land offices is simple: an Indonesian married to a foreigner without a separation of property cannot take Hak Milik or HGB title.

In practice, the problem turns up at the worst possible moment. Developers cancel sales, as Farida found. Banks refuse mortgages: a Hukumonline reader, an Indonesian living in Japan with a Japanese spouse, was asked for a certificate of separation of property before the loan could go ahead, and they did not have one.

## The rule that fixes it

Government Regulation 18 of 2021 (PP 18/2021), Article 70, says an Indonesian citizen married to a foreigner may hold the same land rights as any other Indonesian, provided the land is not joint property, as shown by a separation-of-property agreement between husband and wife made by notarial deed.

Notice what it asks for: a notarial deed made in Indonesia, not a contract signed overseas, and a separation that covers the land. That is why mixed couples who already have a prenup from home are often still advised to make an Indonesian one.

## What changed in 2016

Until 2016, Indonesian law only allowed a marriage agreement to be made before or at the wedding. Couples who had married without one had no way out. Farida's challenge led to Constitutional Court Decision 69/PUU-XIII/2015, read on 27 October 2016, which allows the agreement to be made during the marriage as well.

There are two caveats. The court rejected Farida's challenge to the land law, so the nationality rule on land still stands and the marriage agreement remains the only route. And a postnuptial agreement does not automatically reach back to property you have already bought. Article 29 as amended lets the couple choose when the agreement takes effect, but it cannot harm third parties, such as a bank that already holds security over the property. If you can still sign before the wedding, sign before the wedding.

## What the foreign spouse can own anyway

The foreign partner is not shut out entirely. Under PP 18/2021, a foreigner with a valid Indonesian stay permit may own a house on Hak Pakai (right of use) land, or an apartment unit, above a minimum price the government sets for each region. Residential Hak Pakai can run for up to 80 years across its initial term, extension and renewal. Ask a notary for the current minimum price in your province before you start viewing properties, because the figures have changed several times.

What you should not do is buy through a nominee, meaning an Indonesian who holds the title for you under a side agreement, even when that Indonesian is your own spouse. Article 26(2) of the UUPA makes void any act intended to transfer freehold to a foreigner, directly or indirectly, and the land falls to the state.

## A realistic timeline before a Bali or Jakarta wedding

Most notaries can finish a straightforward agreement in a few weeks. What takes longer is getting the foreign partner's documents into a form an Indonesian notary can use. Counting back from the wedding date:

1. **Three months before: find a notary and gather documents.** The foreign partner needs a passport and, depending on the notary, proof of marital status from home. Documents issued abroad need an apostille, or legalisation for countries outside the Apostille Convention. Indonesia joined the convention with effect from 4 June 2022, so if a guide you are reading still sends you to the embassy, check whether that still applies to your country. Anything not in Indonesian then goes to a sworn translator (penerjemah tersumpah).
2. **Two months before: first consultation and draft.** Bring a list of what each of you owns and owes, in both countries. An agreement involving a foreign party is written in Indonesian and in English or the foreign party's language, as Law 24 of 2009 on language requires. Read the English version, and ask the notary to walk you through the Indonesian text before you sign.
3. **No later than two weeks before: sign in front of the notary.** You both sign the deed before the notary with two witnesses. To count as a prenuptial agreement, it has to be signed before the wedding. If one of you is abroad, ask early whether a power of attorney will work and how it has to be authenticated.
4. **After the wedding: register it.** Once the marriage is recorded, take the deed to wherever it was recorded: the KUA for Muslim couples, the civil registry (Dukcapil) for everyone else. Until then, the agreement binds the two of you but not banks, buyers or the land office.

If you are marrying in Bali with a civil registration the same week, tell both your notary and your wedding planner the date as early as you can. The civil registry appointment and the notary's signing day are the two pieces hardest to move.

## Married abroad?

Couples who married overseas need to register the marriage in Indonesia first, because an agreement can only be recorded against a marriage the state has recorded. The Marriage Law allows one year from the couple's return to Indonesia (Article 56(2)), while the civil registration rules ask for a report within 30 days. The sources we read disagree on which deadline offices actually apply, so report promptly and check with your local Dukcapil.

If you already signed a prenup in your home country, keep it, but don't rely on it for land in Indonesia. A common approach is two linked agreements: one under Indonesian law for assets here, one under foreign law for assets abroad, each referring to the other.

## What a prenup cannot do

- It cannot give the foreign partner land rights. Only the routes above do that.
- It cannot settle child custody in advance. A court decides on the child's interests at the time.
- It cannot waive or rearrange the spouses' rights to inherit from their children, and for Muslim couples it cannot remove the husband's duty to provide for the household.

One note on inheritance. Indonesian courts have ruled both ways on whether a total separation of property also removes the surviving spouse's right to inherit, and the question is now before the Constitutional Court in a case brought by a Japanese widow. We cover it in [lessons from a spouse's inheritance case](https://7magicwedding.com/artikel/tips-hubungan/perjanjian-pranikah-dan-hak-waris-pasangan). Ask your notary about it directly.

## Talking about it with the family

In many Indonesian families a prenup still sounds like planning for divorce, and a suggestion from a foreign future in-law can read as distrust. Yet in a mixed marriage the agreement mainly protects the Indonesian partner, since it is what lets them keep buying land in their own country. That is also the easiest way to explain it to parents.

Let the Indonesian partner raise it with their family, in their own language, with a concrete reason: "Without this, I can't buy a house here in my own name." A sentence like that usually lands faster than an explanation of legal articles.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can we make the agreement after the wedding?

Yes, since the 2016 Constitutional Court decision. Make sure you discuss with the notary when the agreement takes effect and what happens to property bought before the deed is signed.

### Is a prenup from my home country valid in Indonesia?

For land, don't rely on it. PP 18/2021 asks for a separation-of-property agreement made by notarial deed, registered at the KUA or Dukcapil. Make an Indonesian version that refers to the one from home.

### Do we both have to be in Indonesia to sign?

It is easiest if you are. If not, ask the notary early about a power of attorney, since authenticating one abroad takes extra time.

### We only had a religious ceremony. Can the agreement be registered?

Not until the marriage is recorded by the state. Register the marriage at the KUA or Dukcapil first, then the agreement.
