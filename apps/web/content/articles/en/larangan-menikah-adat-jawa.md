The wedding plans are well along, the two families have met, and then a relative suddenly asks, "Which child are you in your family? Which direction is their house from here?" To a couple who did not grow up with Javanese custom, questions like these can feel strange. To a family that keeps the custom, they are how it makes sure no taboo is being broken.

This article sums up the marriage taboos that come up most in Javanese custom, where they come from, the remedies families usually turn to, and how families see them today. One thing to keep in mind from the start: the details of each taboo differ from village to village, and not every Javanese family believes in them.

## Jilu: a first child and a third child

Jilu comes from siji (one) and telu (three). The taboo applies when a first child plans to marry a third child. In popular belief such a couple will have a hard life, with misfortune and trouble coming one after another. Other studies record fears of meagre income, a sickly household, even the death of one of the parents.

In some areas the taboo is also called lusan, and the details are not uniform. A study in Kediri found that jilu applies only when the first child is the woman and the third child the man. The other way round is called lusan and is in fact allowed. Elsewhere the two are treated as the same.

**Remedies families use:** a ruwatan, a ceremony to ward off misfortune, is the way out for families who still want to go ahead. In some villages breaking the taboo without a remedy can leave a family shunned, while in others it is already regarded as a myth.

## Ngalor-ngulon: a home to the north-west

Ngalor-ngulon means to the north and to the west, that is, north-west. The taboo applies when the prospective partner's home lies north-west of one's own family home. Some areas count it only from the groom's house, others in both directions. The fear is that the marriage will not be happy, that there will be constant quarrelling, or that a family member will fall ill or die.

Its origins vary too. In Prambon, Nganjuk, the taboo is linked to a story of Majapahit's grudge against Mataram. The same study records another tale about messengers of Aji Saka who died facing north-west and south-east.

**Remedies families use:** in Prambon several conditions are recorded, such as a siraman tuwuh, avoiding an akad on Mondays and Tuesdays, and a ritual of symbolically "casting out" the bride or groom. In Banyumas, some families choose to hold only the ijab kabul, without a reception. Religious leaders interviewed in a study in Klaten said such a marriage is allowed, because the taboo is not mentioned in the Qur'an, the hadith or the books of fiqh.

## Marrying in the month of Suro

Suro, the first month of the Javanese calendar and the counterpart of Muharram, is generally avoided for celebrations. The reasons given vary:

- Suro is seen as too exalted a month for festivities, and is often called the palace's month.
- One view holds that only the king was considered able to hold a celebration in Suro, so ordinary people avoided it. The word used is avoided, not forbidden.
- Among Nahdliyin, some link it to respect for the death of Sayyidina Husain in the month of Muharram.

Besides Suro, primbon also lists other months to avoid, such as Pasa (Ramadan), Mulud, and Sela or Apit. Jumadilakir, Rejeb, Ruwah and Besar, on the other hand, are considered good months. We have also mapped every Javanese month to its Gregorian dates for 2027 in a separate article.

On the religious side, no verse of the Qur'an and no hadith forbids marrying in any particular month. That is why many families treat month taboos as custom to be respected, not as religious rules.

## Weton combinations considered a poor match

Besides weton jodoh results such as Pegat or Padu, other terms are often heard. One is geyeng, meaning wobbly or ill-fitting. A study in Prambon records a ban on marriage for a weton total of 25 with the pasaran Pahing and Wage. Some people consider geyeng impossible to remedy by any ritual.

Another method divides the total neptu by 10. Remainders of 5, 6 and 7 are considered poor, and each has its remedy: slaughtering a chicken for satriya wirang, mendhem siti or burying earth for bumi kapetak, and scattering earth for lebu katiup angin. ## A younger sibling marrying first

This may be the mildest taboo, because there is almost always a way through it. If a younger sibling marries before an older one, the younger is said to step over the elder, and custom calls for a langkahan ceremony.

According to an explanation from the Yogyakarta Palace, the younger sibling gives a pelangkah gift to the older one who has been stepped over. What it is depends on the older sibling, usually pisang sanggan and a set of clothes, given before the pingitan or siraman and closed with a sungkeman. The ceremony is believed to clear away obstacles. One widely reported example is Erina Gudono, who held a langkahan before marrying Kaesang Pangarep in December 2022.

## How to talk about it with your family

Customary taboos rarely cause problems because of what they say. They cause problems because they surface late or are raised in the wrong tone. A few things help:

1. **Ask early.** Before the formal proposal, ask your parents whether there are any taboos they keep. It is easier to prepare a remedy six months before the wedding than a week before.
2. **Find out your family's version.** Because the details differ by region, the version online may not be the one your grandparents hold.
3. **Bring in an elder.** If the two families see things differently, an elder respected by both sides can often mediate, including deciding which remedy is enough.
4. **Remember that many remedies are simple.** A selamatan, some charity, or moving the day of the akad is often enough to put everyone at ease.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do Javanese marriage taboos have any legal basis?

No. These taboos are custom and belief, not rules in the Marriage Law. A marriage that meets the requirements of religion and is registered with the state is valid, whatever the customary reckoning says.

### How does Islam view these taboos?

Studies in Islamic law generally classify them as 'urf, community custom. Honouring them as an ancestral heritage is considered acceptable, but believing that breaking them will certainly bring misfortune is considered excessive, and even harmful to one's faith.

### What if our parents still refuse because of a customary taboo?

Usually the best route is to find a remedy the family accepts, with an elder's help. If things are truly stuck and a parent refuses to act as wali, Indonesian law provides the wali adhal mechanism through the religious court, but that is a last resort best avoided.

### Do all Javanese people still keep these taboos?

No. Even within the same village, some hold to them seriously and others consider them myths. That is why the conversation with your family matters more than any list.
