A Balinese wedding is called a pawiwahan, and for Balinese Hindus it is not simply a party. The pawiwahan is a manusa yadnya, one of the sacred offerings of the human life cycle, and the doorway into grahasta, the householder stage of life. That is why the ceremony is long and involves two extended families, the family temple and the customary village.

This article sets out the order: what happens at each stage, in whose house, who leads it and what it means. At the end we explain when a Balinese wedding is considered valid, both under custom and under Indonesian law, and a few forms of marriage that are rarely explained, such as nyentana.

One note before we start. Bali has well over a thousand customary villages, each with its own awig-awig, the village's customary rules, and its own habits. The sources we used agree that every area adds ceremonies of its own, and names and order differ between sources. Read the sequence below as a general framework, then ask the family or the local pemangku, the temple priest, for the details.

## The basis: purusa and pradana

Balinese Hindu society is patrilineal, a system called purusa. In the most common form of marriage, the bride leaves her own family and joins the groom's, including its family temple. That is why most of the ceremonies take place at the groom's family home, and why there is a ceremony for the bride to take leave of her own ancestors.

This explains much of what looks unusual to outsiders: why the bride is "fetched", why a yellow cloth is removed from her at the gate of the groom's house, and why, after the wedding, the couple go to the bride's family with offerings to say goodbye.

## 1. Madewasa ayu: choosing the good day

Before any venue booking or invitation, the family first asks for a good day, nunas dewasa, from a sulinggih (high priest), a pemangku or an expert in wariga, the Balinese science of time. This day sets the whole schedule, from the arrival of the groom's family to the main ceremony. Because the Balinese calendar counts several cycles at once, the good day often falls on a weekday.

## 2. Ngidih or memadik: the proposal

Ngidih literally means to ask. The groom's family comes to the bride's family home to propose formally. A marriage preceded by a proposal like this is called memadik or mapadik. It is the most common form and is considered the most honourable.

At the proposal the groom's side brings gifts called **basan pupur**. A study of Balinese bridal dress and ceremony from Universitas Negeri Surabaya records their meaning as respect for the bride, a replacement for her mother's milk, and a replacement for the love of the father who raised her.

## 3. Ngekeb: preparing the bride

Ngekeb is the bride's period of preparation before she is fetched. Outwardly she is cared for, with body scrubs and beauty treatments. Inwardly she prays to Ida Sang Hyang Widhi, God in Balinese Hinduism, for a happy marriage. In many families the bride stays in her room during ngekeb and does not come out until she is fetched.

## 4. Mungkah lawang and the fetching of the bride

Mungkah lawang means opening the door. An envoy of the groom's family knocks on the door of the bride's room, usually to the sound of Balinese song, until the door is opened. The bride is then fetched and taken to the groom's family home.

When she is fetched, the bride wears traditional dress and is veiled from head to foot in a thin yellow cloth. The cloth symbolises that she is leaving her single life behind.

## 5. Mesegeh agung: the welcome at the groom's house

On arrival at the groom's family home, before entering the compound, the couple are welcomed with the mesegeh agung ceremony. The bride's future mother-in-law removes the yellow cloth and exchanges it for uang satakan, a bundle of kepeng coins. The meaning is acceptance: the bride is welcomed into her new world, and her past is left outside the gate.

## 6. Mabyakala or mekala-kalaan: purification

This is one of the most important ceremonies. Mabyakala, also called mekala-kalaan, purifies both bride and groom in body and mind, above all the seeds of life in each of them, so that the child they have will be suputra, of good character. It is led by the officiant, usually a pemangku.

The equipment is extensive and each item has a meaning. Among them are the sanggah surya, the kelabang kala nareswari, the tikeh dadakan (a pandan mat the bride sits on), a kris, the tegen-tegenan (a shoulder load carried by the groom), the dagang-dagangan (a basket carried by the bride), a broom of palm ribs and the benang pepegatan thread. According to one source, the sequence runs roughly like this:

- Prayers and puja by the officiant.
- Burning tetimpug, pieces of bamboo heated until they crack loudly, which the couple then step over.
- Tepung tawar and the byakaon offering.
- The couple touch a raw egg with their toes.
- Pengelukatan, purification with holy water.
- The couple walk around the offerings, the groom carrying the tegen-tegenan and the bride the dagang-dagangan.
- Breaking the benang pepegatan thread, a sign that the couple have entered grahasta.

The order and the meaning of each item differ between sources and between villages. One source mentions a duck egg, another a chicken egg, for example. For the family holding the ceremony, the local pemangku's guidance is what counts.

Mabyakala also brings in one of the three witnesses of a Balinese wedding, explained below.

## 7. Mewidhi widana and mesakapan: validation before the ancestors

After mabyakala, the couple pray at the sanggah or merajan, the family temple of the groom's family. Here the family tells the ancestors that a new member has joined. This ceremony is called mewidhi widana and is led by the family temple's pemangku, or by a sulinggih or ida pedanda at larger ceremonies.

Many sources describe mewidhi widana as the point at which the marriage becomes valid before Ida Sang Hyang Widhi Wasa, custom and society. Some sources, including the Unesa study, call this validating stage **mesakapan**, the ceremony that completes the marriage. The term differs between areas, but the meaning is the same: this is where the marriage is considered complete in religious terms.

## 8. Mepamit and mejauman: saying goodbye to the bride's ancestors

Because the bride now belongs to her husband's family, she needs to take leave of her own family's ancestors. She does so by praying at her family's sanggah, bringing offerings such as ketipat bantal, alem, sumping, cerorot, apem, kuskus, wajik, kekupa, fruit and Balinese dishes.

Sources disagree on the timing here. Some place **mejauman** after mewidhi widana: the married couple come with the groom's family to the bride's family home. Others describe **mepamit** as a ceremony before the wedding and treat mejauman as another name for the same rite. Because every family does it differently, ask early when the leave-taking will happen so it does not clash with anything else on the schedule. The ketipat bantal itself stands for purusa and pradana, the male and female now united.

## 9. The reception

The reception is not part of the religious ceremony. The Unesa study describes it as the events that follow the wedding ceremony. So a reception can be held at the family home or at a hall or hotel, and it does not have to fall on the same day as the customary ceremony.

## When is a Balinese wedding valid?

**In religion and custom.** A Balinese Hindu wedding is validated by three witnesses, called the Tri Upasaksi:

- **Bhuta saksi**, the witness of the lower beings, brought in through the mabyakala ceremony.
- **Dewa saksi**, the witness of the gods, through prayer and the pawiwahan offerings at the sanggah.
- **Manusa saksi**, the human witnesses: the family, the customary village officials and the community present.

**Under Indonesian law.** Under Article 2 of Law No. 1 of 1974 on Marriage, a marriage is valid if it is performed according to the law of the couple's religion, and every marriage is registered according to the regulations. Hindu couples register at the Civil Registry (Disdukcapil), not at the KUA, which handles Muslim marriages. The marriage must be reported to the Civil Registry within 60 days of the ceremony, and the marriage certificate is what you need for a new family card and for your children's birth certificates.

So even a complete customary ceremony does not register the marriage. The documents required can differ between regencies, including a letter from a religious leader or from the customary village officials, so ask the local Disdukcapil for its list well before the day. ## Forms of marriage other than memadik

The order above is that of memadik, marriage by proposal. Balinese customary law recognises other forms.

**Ngerorod or ngerangkat.** A marriage that goes ahead without the prior consent of both families, often called elopement. Unlike the usual picture of elopement, it is the wish of both bride and groom out of mutual love, not one party carrying off the other. The purification ceremony still takes place at the groom's home, and the bride's family is then formally informed. A study in Tabanan records this notification as ngandeg, accompanied by a statement from the bride that she is marrying without coercion.

**Nyentana.** The reverse of the usual pattern: the groom joins the bride's family. In nyentana the wife takes purusa status and the husband pradana. The husband lives with his wife's family and the children follow the mother's line. This form is usually chosen when the bride's family has no son, so one of the daughters is raised to purusa status to carry on the family line. The proposal, too, comes from the bride's family. The position of a nyentana husband towards his own birth family, particularly over inheritance, is still widely debated among legal scholars.

Because the form of marriage decides who joins whose family, it also affects property and inheritance. Couples who want clear rules on what each of them owns, for example because one of them brings family land or a family business, can sign a [marital agreement with a notary in Bali](https://7magicwedding.com/perjanjian-pranikah-bali) before the ceremony.

## How long does a Balinese wedding take?

There is no fixed answer, because it depends on the form of ceremony, caste, the family's means and village custom. The core sequence, from fetching the bride to mewidhi widana, can be completed in a day, while family and neighbours may start preparing the offerings days earlier. Larger ceremonies, for example in a griya (priestly) or puri (royal) household, can run longer.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a Balinese wedding ceremony called?

A pawiwahan. The term covers the whole sequence of a Balinese Hindu wedding, which is a form of manusa yadnya.

### Which ceremonies matter most?

Mabyakala or mekala-kalaan, the purification of the couple, and mewidhi widana or mesakapan, the validation at the family temple. Together they bring in the bhuta and dewa witnesses of the Tri Upasaksi.

### Can a Balinese wedding be simplified?

Yes, and many families do. What is usually kept is the core: purification and validation at the family temple. The reception, the number of offerings and additional rites can be adjusted. That decision is made with the family and the pemangku, not with the wedding planner alone.

### Can a non-Balinese couple have a Balinese wedding?

The pawiwahan is a Hindu religious ceremony. Couples who are not Hindu usually marry according to their own religion and can bring in elements of Balinese culture, such as payas agung bridal dress, gamelan or decoration, without performing the religious rites. ### What should guests prepare?

Modest clothing, and if the ceremony is at a temple or a family compound, a kamen (sarong) and selendang (sash). Our guide on [what to wear to a Bali wedding](https://7magicwedding.com/artikel/beauty-fashion/baju-kondangan-ke-bali-musim-hujan) covers it.
