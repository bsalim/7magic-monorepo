Nearly every Chinese family has one aunt who counts. At the sangjit, the Chinese Indonesian betrothal when the groom's family brings trays of gifts to the bride's, she counts the trays as the procession comes in, counts the oranges on each one, and glances at the figure on the red envelope. If the count is even and the numbers are good, she nods and the day goes on. If not, you'll hear about it, possibly for years.

For couples who grew up speaking Indonesian and no longer use Mandarin or Hokkien at home, the rules about numbers can feel random. They aren't. Almost all of them come from a single source, which is how words sound. Once you see the logic, you don't need to memorise a list of taboos. You can work them out yourself.

## It all starts with sound

Mandarin and the other Chinese languages have a great many words that sound alike but mean very different things. Out of that grew the habit of avoiding numbers that sound like something bad, and seeking out numbers that sound like something good.

| Number | Sound in Mandarin | Sounds like | Feeling |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | er | Tied to the saying that good things come in pairs | Good. A wedding is about a pair |
| 4 | si | si (死), death | Avoided |
| 6 | liu | liu (流), to flow smoothly | Good. Everything goes smoothly |
| 8 | ba | fa (发), to prosper, to grow | Very good |
| 9 | jiu | jiu (久), long-lasting | Very good for a marriage |

The likeness between four and the word for death exists outside Mandarin too. In Hokkien and Cantonese the two words are also close, which is why this taboo is known in nearly every Chinese community in Indonesia. It's the reason many buildings have no fourth floor, and it's the reason sangjit trays aren't arranged in fours.

## Sangjit trays: an even number, and skip four

There are two basic rules. The number of trays must be even, because a wedding is about a couple, and four is skipped. In practice the most common numbers are six, eight and twelve. Eight is the favourite, because of how it sounds.

What goes on the trays follows the same logic. Fruit and cakes are laid out in even numbers, and many families use eighteen for oranges, apples or red steamed cakes. Red candles always come as a pair, usually with a dragon and a phoenix. Even the happiness character you see pasted everywhere is really a disguised number two: 囍 is the character for "joy" written twice side by side, which is why it's called double happiness.

You may have read lists that give four trays as one option, and it's true that some families don't mind. But since you never know who in your future in-laws' extended family is paying attention, six or eight is the route nobody will ever question.

## Red envelopes: milk money, banquet money, and guests' envelopes

The figure inside a red envelope follows the same rules, with one addition. Amounts for a happy occasion are always even. Odd amounts are associated with condolence money, so they aren't used at weddings.

For the **uang susu** ("milk money", thanking the bride's parents for raising her) and the **uang pesta** (a contribution towards the banquet) handed over at the sangjit, the groom's family usually picks an amount that contains eights or nines, for example one ending in 888 or 999. Custom doesn't set the size. That's a matter of means and of what the two families agree, and it's best talked through openly well before the day.

What confuses people is what happens next. In many families the bride's side doesn't keep all of the banquet money. They take only the "tail", meaning the digits at the back of the figure, and return the rest. The message is that the bride's family accepts the goodwill without seeming to sell their daughter. Practice around the milk money varies more. Some families keep it whole as thanks to the mother, and some take half. There isn't one correct version, so ask each set of parents which one they hold to, and settle it before the event.

For envelopes from guests at the reception, the principle is the same: even, and avoid a leading four where you can.

## Dates and times

Choosing the date usually involves the lunar calendar and an almanac called the Tong Shu, often with help from a senior relative or a specialist. One thing that's checked is how the day sits with both partners' zodiac signs. We explain how that works in more detail in our article on choosing a wedding date using the Javanese weton and the Chinese zodiac.

One taboo that's close to universal is the **seventh month of the lunar calendar**, known in Indonesia as Cit Gwee, or ghost month. Many families avoid both sangjit and weddings for that whole month. There's a practical side to this. Dates in that month are usually easier to get at venues, while the months either side are busier.

On the ordinary calendar, eights and nines are sought after, and dates containing a four are sometimes avoided. Doubled dates such as the eighth of the eighth month get booked up quickly.

As for the hour, a sangjit is normally held towards midday, roughly between eleven and one. The usual explanation: the sun is rising, and fortune is hoped to rise with it. That's also why a sangjit nearly always runs on into lunch.

## When the numbers collide with real life

On paper it's all tidy. In actual planning, something nearly always clashes. The venue you want is only free on the fourteenth. The gift budget stretches to seven full trays, or eight that look sparse. Your own parents are relaxed about numbers, while your future in-laws count everything.

Some ways to handle it:

1. **Find out first who actually cares.** Ask both sets of parents whether there are numbers or dates they avoid. It's far better to hear the answer six months before the event than six days.
2. **Separate what's seen from what isn't.** Everyone sees the number of trays. Table numbers, hotel room numbers or the reception's start time rarely get noticed. Follow the rules where it shows, and give yourself some slack elsewhere.
3. **Adjust the contents, never the count.** If money is tight, eight trays with simpler contents is always safer than seven lavish ones. Two trays can hold fruit, which costs the least.
4. **Let an elder decide.** If the two families have different versions, ask one person both sides respect to make the call. Grandpa's ruling is rarely challenged.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does four really have to be avoided in everything?

No. What gets watched most is the number of trays, the amounts in red envelopes, and the date. Beyond that, most families don't fuss. And some families don't mind four at all. Go by your family's habits and your partner's family's. Lists on the internet, this article included, are only a starting point.

### Our family is Protestant or Catholic. Do the number rules still apply?

Many Chinese Christian and Catholic families still follow them as a cultural habit and a way of respecting their parents, without tying them to belief. Others choose to let them go. Both are reasonable. Talk it over with your parents and, if it helps, with your pastor or priest.

### Which is better for a wedding, eight or nine?

Eight stands for prosperity, nine for lasting. For a marriage, nine is actually a very good fit, which is why amounts ending in 99 or 999 are so common. Many families combine the two.

### What if the auspicious date falls on a working day?

It happens a lot. The usual answer is to hold the core ceremony, say the sangjit or the tea pai, on that good date with close family, and have the reception on the nearest weekend. Ask whoever calculated the date which event most needs to fall on the good day.

## Next steps

Start a shared note with your partner containing four lines: the number of trays, the pattern for red-envelope amounts, the dates to avoid, and which elder in each family has the deciding voice. Fill it in after talking to both sets of parents. Every other decision gets much easier from there, because you know exactly what has to be honoured and what's open to negotiation.
