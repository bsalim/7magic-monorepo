Ask Sundanese people whether they have a reckoning of good days like the Javanese, and you may get two answers. Some will say no, Sunda has no such system. Others will tell you about an uncle or village elder who is always consulted before anyone in the family marries.

Both are partly right. The reckoning of good days is less widespread than in Java, but researchers have recorded that in a number of Sundanese villages the tradition is still alive. This article sums up what has been recorded: where the reckoning comes from, how matches and wedding dates are read, and how Sundanese families approach it now.

## Paririmbon and palintangan

Paririmbon is the Sundanese word for notes of knowledge that are hard to memorise, including the reckoning of days. It comes from imbu, meaning to store. Paririmbon is related to the Javanese primbon, and many dictionaries treat the two as the same thing.

The reckoning of good days itself is known as palintangan. Its result is a poé alus, a good day, or a poé naas, an unlucky one. The person doing the reckoning is usually not the couple themselves but a canoli, an elder or a customary leader.

## Naktu values in the Sundanese reckoning

Like neptu in Javanese primbon, paririmbon gives every day a value. The value is called naktu. For the days of the week, the values are the same as those used in Java: Ahad 5, Senén 4, Salasa 3, Rebo 7, Kemis 8, Jumaah 6 and Saptu 9.

The pasaran are the same too, though one name differs slightly. Legi in Javanese is called Manis in Sundanese.

| Pasaran | Naktu |
|---|---|
| Manis | 5 |
| Pahing | 9 |
| Pon | 7 |
| Wagé | 4 |
| Kaliwon | 8 |

A study in Sukabumi Regency also records values for each month and year. Asked where the values came from, the informant simply answered "tos tidituna", it has always been so.

## Two recorded ways of matching a couple

There is no single standard Sundanese formula for a match. What exists are several methods recorded by researchers from particular informants, and they differ from one another. We present the two examples below as records, not as a calculator.

**In Sukabumi**, researchers recorded two approaches. The first uses birth data: the naktu of the day, pasaran, month and year are added and divided by five. The remainder is read in order as Sri, Lungguh, Dunya, Lara and Pati. The second approach uses the names of the bride and groom. Each letter of a name is given a value from the order of the Sundanese script, then the values of both names are added and divided by five.

**In Banjar**, a canoli interviewed by researchers from Universitas Siliwangi also uses names, but with a different letter table and a divisor of seven. The result is read as Sri, Lungguh, Dunya, Lara, Pati, Mineus and Pleus. According to this informant, the ideal couple is one whose count lands exactly on Pleus.

The differences in divisor, table and result names point to one thing: paririmbon is passed from person to person, and every reckoner may have their own version. If your family has an elder who usually does the reckoning, that elder's method is the one used.

## Choosing the wedding date and month

For the wedding date, the canoli in Banjar divides the Hijri date by five and reads the remainder in the same order, from Sri to Pati. He also named several prohibitions:

- **Months to avoid:** Muharram, Safar and Mulud, which he said must never be used for a wedding.
- **Days to avoid in each month:** in Syawal, Hapit and Rayagung, Friday is avoided. In Muharram, Safar and Mulud, Saturday and Sunday. In Silih Mulud and the two Jumadil months, Monday and Tuesday. In Rajab, Rewah and Puasa, Wednesday and Thursday.

Note that these are one informant's rules, not rules for all of Sunda.

One more widely known taboo is marrying in the month of **Safar**, which according to detikJabar is considered taboo by some Sundanese people. The same article explains that Islam knows no bad luck in Safar, and that several marriages in Islamic history in fact took place in that month.

## Who does the reckoning, and when

In the customary village of Cikondang, Bandung Regency, a 2025 study records that wedding matters, from the choice of partner to the day of the celebration, are still reckoned by the village elder. The prospective couple is first brought before the elder, and only then is the day set.

The reckoning usually runs alongside the stages of a Sundanese wedding: neundeun omong as the families' first agreement, ngalamar, then seserahan, before the siraman and the akad.

Interestingly, the belief is often held loosely. Researchers in Sukabumi recorded the term babaledogan, which means roughly a gamble. If the result is good, be grateful. If not, be patient.

## If your partner comes from another tradition

Many weddings in West Java bring a Sundanese family together with a Javanese, Chinese Indonesian or other family. Because the Sundanese naktu for days and pasaran match the Javanese neptu, the two families can often use the same language of reckoning, even if they read the results differently. For comparison, you can read how Javanese families work out [weton jodoh](https://7magicwedding.com/artikel/tradisi-wedding/weton-jodoh-menghitung-kecocokan-pasangan-primbon-jawa) and the [marriage taboos in Javanese custom](https://7magicwedding.com/artikel/tradisi-wedding/larangan-menikah-adat-jawa).

In practice, families from two traditions usually look for a date that passes both reckonings. If there is none, elders from both sides mediate.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is paririmbon the same as Javanese primbon?

They are related. The values for days and pasaran are the same, and the terms are similar. The difference lies in how results are read and in the oral tradition handed down in each area.

### Do Sundanese families have to reckon a good day before marrying?

No. Some families still keep it, especially in customary villages, while many others no longer use it. What counts is each family's own habit.

### What does Islam say about choosing a month to marry?

NU Online explains that there is no prohibition on marrying in any particular month. Choosing a day by custom is allowed as long as one believes that God determines everything.

### Is the month of Hapit forbidden for weddings in Sundanese custom?

The ban on marrying in the month of Hapit or Apit is recorded mostly in areas of Javanese culture. In the record from Banjar, Hapit is not forbidden; only Fridays in that month are avoided.
