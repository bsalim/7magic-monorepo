On 23 September 1997, the day before her wedding, Hisako Dewanto signed a marriage agreement before a notary in Surabaya. She was a Japanese citizen in her early twenties, and her Indonesian was still limited. At the Constitutional Court she said her husband's family and a company staff member had told her the agreement was needed because of the land ownership rules and because her husband needed bank credit.

Twenty-five years later her husband, Rachmat Hartojo, died. The courts, all the way up to cassation, ruled that their three children were his heirs with a third each, and that Hisako was not. The reason: the separation-of-property agreement she had signed in 1997.

"I didn't sign to give up any rights, and that's what shocked me after this happened," she told the constitutional judges on 28 September 2026.

This article takes no side in the family's dispute. We put it together from the petition and hearing transcripts published by the Constitutional Court, because the case raises a question couples rarely ask when they sign a prenup: does separate property also mean a separate inheritance?

## The timeline in brief

- **24 September 1997.** Hisako and Rachmat married, and the marriage was entered at the civil registry on 6 October 1997. Marriage agreement deed No. 51 was made the day before the wedding and registered at the Surabaya civil registry.
- **9 October 2022.** Rachmat died. In November 2022 a notary issued a certificate of inheritance giving Hisako and the three children a quarter each.
- **February 2023.** The eldest child sued the notary and Hisako at the Surabaya District Court (case 204/Pdt.G/2023/PN Sby).
- **29 November 2023.** The district court annulled the certificate and named the three children as heirs, a third each. The ruling was upheld on appeal in February 2024 and in cassation on 28 November 2024.
- **7 September 2026.** Hisako and three other petitioners filed a judicial review of Article 140 of the Civil Code (KUHPerdata) at the Constitutional Court (case 334/PUU-XXIV/2026). The preliminary hearing was on 15 September and the hearing on the revised petition on 28 September.

Several outlets reported the estate as worth hundreds of billions of rupiah. That figure comes from the petitioners' lawyers, based on tax data, not from any court's valuation, and it has not been verified.

## Why separate property can end in losing an inheritance

Under the Civil Code, which governs many non-Muslim families, two different rights come into play when a husband or wife dies.

The first is a share of joint property. Without an agreement, half the marital property already belongs to the surviving spouse, quite apart from any inheritance. A separation-of-property agreement removes this share, and that is exactly what it is for.

The second is the right to inherit. Article 832 of the Civil Code names the surviving spouse as an heir, and Article 852a gives them the same share as a legitimate child. This is what is in dispute in Hisako's case: does a separation-of-property agreement also remove the second right?

Hisako's lawyers argue that it does not. In their view, a marriage agreement decides *what* goes into the estate, not *who* is entitled to inherit it. The Supreme Court once ruled along those lines in case 804 PK/Pdt/2009, and an academic note from Universitas Indonesia called the annulment of the inheritance certificate in Hisako's case mistaken. The final rulings in her own case, however, said otherwise.

The article under review, Article 140 of the Civil Code, says a marriage agreement may not reduce "the rights the law grants to the longest-surviving spouse". The petitioners ask the Court to read that phrase explicitly: a total separation-of-property agreement does not remove the surviving spouse's standing as an heir, including a spouse of foreign nationality, while land law still applies.

## What is happening at the Constitutional Court

Besides Hisako there are three other petitioners. One is a university student who may one day marry under a separation-of-property agreement. The other two are a married couple who wed in 2008 and signed a postnuptial separation agreement in 2024. They fear the same thing could happen to them.

The panel, chaired by Constitutional Justice Enny Nurbaningsih, raised several points. Justice Enny noted that under the Court's earlier decisions only Indonesian citizens have standing to challenge a law, and that judicial review does not settle a concrete case. Justice Arsul Sani questioned whether Article 140 of the Civil Code still applies now that the Marriage Law exists.

As of early October 2026 the case has passed the hearing on the revised petition and is waiting for the judges' deliberation meeting. There is no ruling yet.

## Lessons before you sign a prenup

**Don't sign the day before the wedding.** Prenups are often rushed into the last week, between dress fittings and vendor meetings. Give yourselves at least a few weeks to read the draft, ask questions and ask for changes.

**Make sure you actually understand the language.** For an agreement with a foreign party, Law 24 of 2009 asks for it to be written in Indonesian and in the foreign party's language or English. Ask for that version, and ask the notary to read it out and explain it, not just to have you initial every page.

**Ask about inheritance directly.** Almost no couple does, because a prenup is seen as being about property during your lifetimes. Ask the notary: if one of us dies, is the survivor still an heir? If the notary thinks it is needed, ask for this to be stated plainly in the deed, for example that the agreement only separates property and is not intended to waive any inheritance rights. Hisako's case shows that a court can read an agreement that is silent on this in ways you don't expect.

**Consider a will.** A prenup governs property while you are both alive. A will made before a notary governs what happens afterwards, within the reserved share the law protects for children. The two can complement each other, especially in a marriage with children from an earlier marriage or a family business.

**For Muslim couples, the rules are different.** This case concerns the Civil Code. Inheritance in Muslim families generally follows the Compilation of Islamic Law (KHI) and is settled at the Religious Court, with its own rules on a husband's or wife's share. Ask your notary which rules apply to your family.

**For a foreign spouse, remember the one-year rule.** Even if their right to inherit is recognised, a foreigner who inherits freehold (Hak Milik) land without a will must give it up within a year, under Article 21(3) of the Basic Agrarian Law. Justice Arsul Sani raised this at the hearing: the legal right does not disappear, but the land has to be released. Hisako's own lawyer said his client only wants to keep her home. Plan from the start how the foreign spouse will still have somewhere to live if the Indonesian spouse dies first.

**Talk it through with the family while everyone is still here.** The bitterest inheritance disputes happen between people who love each other, and they start from documents nobody understood together. Keep copies of the deed, keep a record of your assets, and make sure your adult children know what their parents signed and why.

## What is still uncertain

The Constitutional Court has not ruled, and it may not reach the substance at all because of the standing issue. Until it does, Indonesian courts have answered the same question in two different directions. That is the strongest reason to make your own agreement as explicit as possible.
