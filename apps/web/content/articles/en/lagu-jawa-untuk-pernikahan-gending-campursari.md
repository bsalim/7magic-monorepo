Music at a traditional Javanese wedding really comes in two halves, and different people decide each one. The first is the gending, the gamelan pieces that accompany the rites. That choice is nearly always left to the pambiwara, the master of ceremonies who leads the ritual in Javanese, and to the gamelan group, while the couple simply nod along. The second is the entertainment during the reception. That part is entirely yours, and it often only gets discussed a week before the day with a keyboard-and-singer act whose repertoire is already fixed.

Both are worth understanding. The first, so you know what's being played as you walk towards your partner. The second, so that no song about a lover who left from a train station starts up at the exact moment you step onto the wedding dais.

## The gending that accompany the rites

Every stage of the panggih, the ritual first meeting of bride and groom, has its own gending. The order below is the one commonly used in the Surakarta style. The Yogyakarta style, and the habits of individual gamelan groups, can differ in places, so treat this table as a conversation starter with your pambiwara.

| Stage of the ceremony | Usual gending | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| The groom's party arrives, guests are welcomed | Kebo Giro | The wedding gending most people recognise. Bold and cheerful |
| The bride walks to her seat | Ladrang Sekartejo | |
| The groom walks to the meeting point | Ladrang Wilujeng | Wilujeng means safe and well. It's a prayer for safety |
| Bride and groom are brought together | Ketawang Kodok Ngorek | The heart of the panggih, played as the two meet |
| The parents lead the couple to the dais | Ketawang Larasmaya | |
| Sungkeman | Ketawang Mijil Wigaringtyas | Slow and hushed. The part that most often sets the guests crying |
| The couple return after changing outfits, the procession | Ketawang Subakastawa | |
| Guests take their leave | Ladrang Gleyong | |

These names are useful for one practical reason. When the pambiwara asks whether you have any requests, you can answer with more than "whatever you think". You might ask for Kodok Ngorek to be played in full and not cut short, or make sure the gending for the sungkeman, when you kneel to ask your parents' blessing, doesn't stop before both sets of parents are done.

## Live gamelan or a recording

In Solo and Yogya, bringing in a full gamelan with a dozen or more musicians is still ordinary. Jakarta is another story. Many venues don't have the floor space or the loading access, and plenty of Javanese receptions end up using recorded gending operated by the pambiwara's team.

A recording isn't a bad option, but it has one weakness: it can't wait. A panggih almost never runs to time. Fabric snags, the photographer asks for a retake, the bride's mother needs a moment to compose herself. Musicians playing live will stretch or slow a gending to follow what's happening. A recording will simply run out halfway, and then there's a silent gap while the operator hunts for the next file.

If you're using recordings, ask the pambiwara's team to prepare long versions of the panggih and sungkeman gending, and make sure one person's only job is the music player. A middle path that more couples are choosing is a small gamelan, sometimes called cokekan, with five or six players. It fits in a corner of a ballroom and the music still breathes.

## Songs for the reception: langgam, campursari, Javanese pop

Once the rites are over and guests are queuing to congratulate you, the mood can lighten. There are three layers of songs to draw on, from the most classic to the newest.

**Langgam and Javanese keroncong.** *Yen Ing Tawang Ana Lintang* and *Nyidam Sari*, both by Andjar Any, are love songs known across generations. The first is about waiting for a sweetheart under a sky full of stars. The second is about a longing that won't ease. The grandparents in the front row usually sing along under their breath.

**Campursari.** This blend of gamelan with keyboard and guitar was born for family celebrations, so almost every keyboard-and-singer act knows it well. *Prau Layar* by Ki Nartosabdo is cheerful and carries no lyrical baggage whatsoever, which makes it good for lifting the room.

**New Javanese pop.** Denny Caknan's generation brought Javanese-language songs to younger guests. *Widodari*, which he performs with Guyon Waton, is about someone head over heels who compares their sweetheart to an angel. *Ojo Dibandingke* is fast, and it's a plea not to be compared with anyone else because there's only one name in the singer's heart. Both are safe to sing in front of the couple.

Be careful with *Kalih Welasku*. It's often requested for receptions because the title means "my compassion" and the melody is gentle, but the story is about a couple who were together for a long time and finally split up. If you love it, treat it like the songs in the next section.

## Songs to skip, or to move to a different hour

**Lingsir Wengi.** This song is a victim of misunderstanding. There's an old devotional chant attributed to Sunan Kalijaga, a prayer for protection through the night. There's also a campursari song of the same name by Sukap Jiman from the 1990s, which is actually a love song. Neither has anything to do with summoning spirits. But ever since the tune was used in the horror film *Kuntilanak*, half your guests will get goosebumps at the first line. Your wedding day isn't the time to set music history straight.

**The heartbreak hits.** Didi Kempot turned heartbreak into something you can dance to, and that's exactly the trap. *Cidro* is about being betrayed after a promise. *Stasiun Balapan* is about a sweetheart who left by train and never came back. *Pamer Bojo* is about an ex who turns up to show off a new partner. From the next generation, Denny Caknan's *Kartonyono Medot Janji* is a song about a broken promise, and *Mendung Tanpo Udan* is about plans for a life together that fell through.

We're not going to tell you these songs are forbidden. At plenty of receptions *Pamer Bojo* is the one that gets guests up and dancing, and that's a joy. What needs managing is the timing. Put them in the entertainment set towards the end, once the couple have come down from the dais and it really is a party. Don't let them turn up as you walk in, during the sungkeman, or during the family photos.

## How to brief the singer and the keyboard player

Singers who work family celebrations have a go-to list, and with no direction they'll perform whatever guests request most. That means the heartbreak hits. Give them two short lists in writing: five to eight songs you're asking for, and the songs that mustn't be performed before a certain hour.

Also spell out what to do when a guest comes up to the stage and asks for a song. At a Javanese celebration that's close to certain. The easiest agreement: guest requests are taken after the family photos are finished.

## Frequently asked questions

### Half our guests aren't Javanese. Will Javanese songs bore them?

The gending during the rites is actually a draw for guests who have never seen a panggih. For the reception, just alternate: two Javanese songs, two Indonesian ones. New Javanese pop like *Ojo Dibandingke* is already well known far beyond Java.

### Can the rites use gending while the reception has a regular pop band?

Yes, and it's the most common arrangement in Jakarta. Just make sure there's a clear pause between the two. Going from a Ketawang straight into a Top 40 song sounds like someone hit the wrong button.

### We're following the Yogyakarta style. Are the gending the same?

Some are, and some differ in name or treatment. A pambiwara and musicians who work in the Yogya style will know the conventions. What matters is that one style is used consistently from start to finish, the same as with the makeup and the outfits.

### Can gending be replaced with modern instrumental music?

Nothing forbids it, but ask your parents first. For many families the sound of gamelan at the panggih is part of the ceremony itself, on a par with the outfits and the offerings.

## Next steps

When you meet your pambiwara, bring the gending table above and ask which order they normally use. Ask, too, whether the accompaniment is live or recorded, and who operates it. For the reception, write those two song lists and send them to the singer at least two weeks before the event. If you don't yet know the order of the rites yourself, read our article on the terms and stages of a traditional Javanese wedding first, so these gending names have somewhere to sit in your head.
