*Xiao Xing Yun* is one of the most requested Mandarin songs for a wedding entrance. The melody is sweet, the title means "a little luck", and the opening line is about how lucky I was to meet you. The trouble is the very next line: but I've already lost the right to cry over you. It's a song about someone who only realises who they loved after that person has gone.

Guests who don't speak Mandarin will never know. But at a Chinese family wedding there are almost always a few relatives who do, and they tend to be sitting at the front tables. The list below is here so you know what is actually being sung before you decide.

## How to use this list

Every song is given in pinyin and in Chinese characters. The pinyin makes it easy to say the title to your band or MC. The characters are for searching on streaming services, because one song's romanised spelling can show up three or four different ways and the results get messy. Just copy the characters.

The lyrics column is a summary only. A word-for-word translation would be longer and more nuanced. If a song is going to be performed live in front of the whole family, ask one fluent relative to read the full lyrics first.

A few notes on that list. The two Teresa Teng songs will be recognised by virtually every guest over fifty, and that alone is reason enough to include them. The melody of *Tian Mi Mi* actually comes from an Indonesian folk song, *Dayung Sampan*, so even guests with no Chinese background often feel they've heard it before.

*Qing Fei De Yi* works differently. Anyone who was a teenager in the early 2000s and watched Meteor Garden will turn their head the moment that guitar intro starts. It's a good one for warming up the tables of the couple's friends.

*Peng You* isn't about romance at all. It belongs at the very end, when some guests are already on their feet and the MC invites the whole room to sing.

## Four songs that turn out to be about a breakup

All four are popular, easy on the ear, and regulars on request lists. All four are about losing someone.

**Xiao Xing Yun** (小幸运) by Hebe Tien is the theme song of the film *Our Times*. The narrator looks back on her school days and realises the person who was always beside her was the piece of luck she let slip.

**Hou Lai** (后来) by Rene Liu means "later". The best-known part goes roughly: later, I finally learned how to love; it's a pity you were long gone by then. It's adapted from a Japanese song by Kiroro, and in many countries it's the default karaoke pick for the broken-hearted.

**Gei Wo Yi Ge Li You Wang Ji** (给我一个理由忘记) by A-Lin means "give me one reason to forget". The title says it all, but because the chorus is so grand, it still gets chosen for processionals.

**Ke Xi Bu Shi Ni** (可惜不是你) by Fish Leong means "what a pity it isn't you", as in, it isn't you who stayed with me to the end. Sung from a wedding stage, that line lands strangely on anyone who understands it.

So what about **Tong Hua**? The lyrics themselves are hopeful. A man promises to be his girlfriend's guardian angel and tells her that happy endings do exist. What makes some people hesitate is the music video, which tells the story of a girlfriend who is gravely ill. We think the song is still worth playing. If a relative remembers the video and isn't comfortable, just move it to dinner and don't use it for the entrance.

## Mapping songs to the running order

**The tea ceremony.** People are speaking softly, cups are changing hands, and relatives are being called up one by one. Use an instrumental version of *Yue Liang Dai Biao Wo De Xin* or another Teresa Teng song. Vocals will clash with the voice of whoever is guiding the ceremony.

**The entrance.** You need a song with a clear intro and a tempo you can walk to. *Jin Tian Ni Yao Jia Gei Wo* gives you both. Ask the sound operator to start from the right spot, because the intro on the original recording is fairly long.

**First dance or cake cutting.** *Wo Yuan Yi* and *Zui Lang Man De Shi* are both slow and both say exactly the right thing. Pick one. Two slow songs back to back and guests start reaching for their phones.

**Dinner.** Here you're free to mix. Alternate Mandarin songs with Indonesian and Western ones so every table gets its turn.

## If your band doesn't sing in Mandarin

This is common, and there are a few ways round it. The simplest is to play the original recording for the moments that matter most, and let the band fill the rest with their own repertoire. The second is to ask the band for an instrumental version. A Teresa Teng melody still comes through without the words.

The third is a guest singer, very often someone in the family. Nearly every extended family has one uncle or aunt with a good voice who has been waiting for exactly this. Tell the band well ahead so they can prepare an arrangement or a backing track, and ask for a short run-through before guests arrive.

What's best avoided is pushing a singer who doesn't speak Mandarin to perform from memorised sounds. Mandarin is tonal, and a tone that slips can change what a word means. Relatives who understand will be more bothered by that than by having no Mandarin song at all.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many Mandarin songs is right for one reception?

It depends on who's in the room. For a mixed guest list, four to six songs placed at the key moments already feels like enough. If nearly all the guests are Chinese and there are a lot of older relatives, it can be half the playlist.

### My partner isn't Chinese. Will Mandarin songs feel one-sided?

Balance it at moments of equal weight. If the entrance uses a Mandarin song, pick one from your partner's background for the cake cutting or the first dance. Families notice this kind of balance more than you'd think.

### Can we still play Xiao Xing Yun if we both genuinely love it?

Of course. It's your wedding. Just put it in the dinner set as one song among many, and don't make it the soundtrack to a spotlight moment. In the middle of a long playlist, nobody will mind.

### Are there Hokkien or Cantonese songs that work?

Yes, and they're usually the ones that reach the grandparents most. *Ke Au* (家后) by Jody Chiang, about a wife staying with her husband into old age, is the one people ask for most.

## Next steps

Choose the three moments that matter most to you, then pick one song for each from the table above. Copy the characters, send them to your band or sound operator along with a link to the original recording, and ask them plainly which ones they can perform live and which are better played from the recording. The rest can be filled in later. Those three moments are the ones that will end up in your wedding film.
