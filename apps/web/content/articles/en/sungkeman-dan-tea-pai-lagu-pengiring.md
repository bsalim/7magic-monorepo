In one room the gamelan slows, and a bride and groom in kebaya and batik shuffle forward on their knees towards their parents' chairs. In another room there's a red teapot and small cups on a tray, and a bride and groom kneel on cushions, lifting the tea with both hands. The clothes are different, the language is different, the objects are different.

What happens in those two rooms is the same. A grown-up child lowers themselves before the people who raised them, the room falls suddenly quiet, and someone in the back row starts looking for a tissue. The sungkeman and the tea pai are what families remember most from a wedding, often more than the vows themselves. And because the room is that quiet, the wrong music is very easy to hear.

## What's the same, what's different

Both are an expression of devotion and a request for a blessing. Both go in order of seniority. And in both, the first one to cry is very often the father.

The difference is in the form. In the **sungkeman**, the Javanese rite of kneeling to ask your parents' forgiveness and blessing, nothing changes hands. The couple kneel, kiss their parents' knees or hands, and whisper their apologies and thanks. The parents answer with a prayer, often stroking their child's head or shoulder. The circle is small: both sets of parents, sometimes the grandparents.

In the **tea pai**, the Chinese tea ceremony, a cup of tea carries the message. The couple serve the tea, the elder drinks it, then gives a red envelope or jewellery along with a few words of advice. The circle is much wider. After parents and grandparents come uncles, aunts and married older siblings, one pair at a time in order of seniority. In a big family, a tea pai can take more than an hour.

That difference feeds straight into the music. A sungkeman is short and concentrated, so one well-chosen piece is enough. A tea pai is long and repetitive, so you need a supply of music that can flow without wearing on the ear.

## Why the music for this moment needs its own plan

Three things set this moment apart from the rest of the day.

First, people are speaking softly. The couple's whispers to their parents are the heart of the sungkeman, and the elders' advice is the heart of the tea pai. A song with lyrics at even a moderate volume is enough to drown them.

Second, the length is hard to predict. Some mothers hold their child for a full two minutes. Some grandfathers give long advice. Music that runs out mid-embrace leaves an awkward hole of silence, and a next track that suddenly arrives with a drum intro is worse still.

Third, people listen to the words. During dinner nobody follows the lyrics. Here everyone is silent and every ear is open.

## Music for the sungkeman

If your ceremony has a gamelan, the usual piece for the sungkeman is **Ketawang Mijil Wigaringtyas**. It's slow and hushed. Musicians playing live will keep repeating it until the last parent is done. It's the safest choice, because there are no lyrics competing with your whispers.

For a reception without gamelan, there's plenty to choose from among Indonesian songs:

- **Bunda** by Potret, written by Melly Goeslaw. Almost everyone knows it by heart, which is exactly why it hits so hard. A piano or violin version often suits the moment better than the sung one.
- **Ayah** by Rinto Harahap. An older song that goes straight to the parents' generation.
- **Yang Terbaik Bagimu** by ADA Band and Gita Gutawa, also known by its subtitle, Jangan Lupakan Ayah. It's sung from a daughter's point of view, so it suits a bride's sungkeman to her father.
- **Titip Rindu Buat Ayah** by Ebiet G. Ade. Beautiful, but heavy. Think about your family's circumstances before choosing it.

Other traditions across the archipelago have songs that do the same job. At a Sundanese wedding, **Indung** commonly accompanies the sungkeman. At a Batak wedding, **Borhat Ma Dainang** is a mother's message as she sends her daughter off to a new household. If one of you comes from that background, this one song will mean more to the family than ten popular ones.

## Music for the tea pai

Because a tea pai is long, build the music in two layers. The first is instrumental for nearly the whole duration: guzheng, erhu, or piano versions of classic Mandarin songs like *Yue Liang Dai Biao Wo De Xin*. The second is one or two sung songs, played specifically when the parents are called forward.

For that second layer, these are the ones couples use most:

- **Gan En De Xin** (感恩的心) by Ouyang Feifei, meaning "a grateful heart". The chorus thanks the person who has stayed beside you through life. Many Chinese Indonesian families know it from school, church or temple events.
- **Fu Qin** (父亲) by Chopsticks Brothers, meaning "father". The music video follows a father through his daughter's childhood, right up to walking her into her wedding.
- **Shi Jian Dou Qu Na Er Le** (时间都去哪儿了) by Wang Zhengliang, meaning "where did the time go". It's about parents growing old while their child is busy growing up.
- **Shi Shang Zhi You Mama Hao** (世上只有妈妈好), "only Mum is the best in the world". A children's song everybody knows. The slow instrumental version sounds sweet. The original sung version can feel a little too childlike for this occasion.

For grandparents who speak Hokkien day to day, **Ke Au** (家后) by Jody Chiang almost always lands. It's about a wife who promises to stay with her husband into old age. Playing it as Grandpa and Grandma sit side by side, taking tea from their grandchild, is one of the moments that most often has the whole room welling up.

## If you're doing both

Chinese–Javanese weddings, or Chinese–Sundanese ones, are more and more common, and many couples choose to hold both a sungkeman and a tea pai. A few things to think through:

Keep them apart in time. Two tearful moments in a row will drain everyone, the parents most of all. A common arrangement is the tea pai in the morning with immediate family, then the sungkeman as part of the afternoon or evening rites.

Give each its own musical colour. If the tea pai uses guzheng and Mandarin songs, let the sungkeman have gamelan or Indonesian songs. Each family will feel its tradition has been honoured in full.

Explain things to the family who are new to it. Parents on the Javanese side may never have received tea while seated and handed over a red envelope. Parents on the Chinese side may feel awkward when their new son- or daughter-in-law kisses their knee. Five minutes of explanation from the MC or wedding organiser beforehand puts everyone at ease.

## Briefing the band and the sound operator

Put these four things in writing. The music starts when the first parent sits down, not when the couple kneel. The piece is looped or blended into the next with no gap until the MC signals the end. Transitions are done by fading the volume down slowly, never by cutting. And no loud intros.

Talk about the microphone as well. Some families want the whispered words of the sungkeman to be heard by the guests, and others consider them private. Decide with your parents first, then tell the MC, so no microphone gets thrust forward unexpectedly.

## Frequently asked questions

### One of our parents has passed away. How do we choose the music?

Avoid songs that name a father or mother specifically, because they can weigh heavily on the parent who is still here, and on you. Instrumental is the gentlest choice. If an uncle, aunt or older sibling is standing in, the order and the form stay the same.

### Can we use Western songs?

Yes. Plenty of couples use instrumental versions of Western songs their family loves. The same considerations apply: a slow tempo, no loud intro, and lyrics that don't clash with the moment.

### How much music should we prepare?

For a sungkeman with two sets of parents, prepare ten to fifteen minutes. For a tea pai, count the number of pairs who'll receive tea, multiply by roughly three minutes, and add a reserve. A big family with twenty pairs of relatives means an hour of music.

### Do the guests need to watch?

Not necessarily. Many families hold the tea pai privately in the morning, and some prefer the sungkeman before guests arrive. Even when it's private you'll still want music, because this moment will be in your wedding film.

## Next steps

Ask your parents one simple question: is there a song that reminds them of when you were small? The answer is often the best choice of all, far better than any list. Then decide whether the moment is open or private, work out how long it will run, and send written instructions to the band or sound operator.
